

# Quillquest: Rules v4
 · 

## About this version
v4 is a clean, playable rule set built from v2 and v3. Every rule here is meant to be used at the first playtest; anything held back is listed in Section 13.
Source: v2 consolidated rules (2026-10-05) plus the v3 design pass, with the v3 review findings fixed.
No inline change markers. The Appendix lists every editor's decision from v3 to v4, with the reason, so Cliff can reverse any of them.
Biggest structural changes: players speak only in named ranks while the software rolls flat dice behind them, with the dice revealed on demand. Shaped curves, the remembering Danger, and growing Belief backing are held back for a later version (Section 13).
Design principle carried through every section: the Notes pane is the writer's prompt. Each mechanic should end in a line a writer can write from.
Working names (Beliefs, Instincts, Traits, Let It Ride, Push, Danger) are temporary; see Section 14.

## 1. Play Format
The game is played in a text window with two panes. The Story pane holds the prose; the Notes pane holds the mechanics and tells the next writer what to write.

### 1.1 Story pane (left)
Players take turns writing prose. Each post is written from one point of view:
the writer's own character;
a borrowed point of view (another player's character); or
the GM's third-person perspective.

### 1.2 Notes pane (right)
The software keeps three sections:

### 1.3 The Notes pane is the writer's prompt
Every roll produces a prompt card in the Ledger. The roll runs at one of two speeds.
Big rolls (a Belief on the line, another character directly affected, or a battle roll) stop the story at the moment of action:
The acting writer posts up to the moment of action and stops. The post shows what the character does, not whether it works.
The writer fills the card: one line of Want, one line of Risk.
The GM answers Set on the card, in one line.
The software rolls and adds the outcome prompt (Section 3.5).
The next post writes the outcome. It must make the outcome prompt true.
Quick rolls (everything else) run inside one post:
The writer fills the card and proposes the ranks: "Trained climber, Ordinary wall. Real Danger: I twist an ankle."
The GM may change the ranks before the roll. If the GM doesn't respond, they stand.
The software rolls, and the writer writes the attempt and its outcome in one post.
GM retcon. If a quick roll doesn't fit the GM's orchestration, the GM may back it up and retcon the result. A retcon happens before the next post builds on that result.

### 1.4 Turn order
Spotlight passing. Every post ends by passing the next post to someone: the character most affected by it, or, if no one is, whoever the writer chooses. The GM may take any post to frame a scene. Anyone left out for two rounds gets the next post.

### 1.5 Borrowed point of view
Anyone may borrow another character's eyes, words, and actions without asking.
The owner may veto any thought, feeling, or choice written for their character. A veto is one line in the Ledger; the writer revises that sentence.
A borrowed post can never make a character break a Belief or Instinct. Only the owner writes that.
A borrowed post can't start a roll for the borrowed character. If that character's action needs a roll, the writer stops at the moment of action and the owner fills the card. Borrowing gives you the camera, not the dice.

## 2. Design Goals
The top three goals decide every conflict between goals.
Top priorities
Put character beliefs, relationships, history, and difficult choices at the center of play.
Players know what they are trying to accomplish and what they are risking before they roll.
Failure stays meaningful even for highly capable characters.
Supporting goals
A relevant Belief always backs you, never guarantees you.
Every roll risks something. If nothing is at risk, don't roll.
Beliefs tested at real cost change the character in lasting ways.
Avoid Burning Wheel's bookkeeping. The software keeps the records; players keep the story.
One core mechanic for physical, social, combat, wounds, healing, magic, and resources.
Character creation produces biography, relationships, position, unfinished business, resources, setting, and hooks.
Injuries and Burdens shape how a character plays; they never stop them playing.
Magic is dangerous, flexible, and tied to character history.
Prefer named fictional facts over numeric modifiers.
Favor mechanics that generate story over mechanics that simulate process.
Every roll reads at a glance in words and can be said in one sentence of fiction; the dice stay one tap away.

## 3. The Roll
Every roll tests the character's Skill against two things: Difficulty decides whether you get what you want, and Danger decides whether the declared cost lands. Players speak only in named ranks; the software rolls the dice behind them.

### 3.1 When to roll
Roll only when there is a goal and a Danger. If nothing is at risk, don't roll.
A mundane task can still carry Danger through its consequences: the library search that turns up the wrong name.
If success is certain for someone of this Skill, don't roll.

### 3.2 The prompt card
The quick form is the default. For a quick roll the player writes it, proposing the ranks (Section 1.3); for a big roll the GM answers it. One line covers the whole card:
Climb the wall. Capable climber against a Demanding wall. Real Danger: I twist an ankle.
The full card is used when the stakes need discussion or someone disputes the call:
Check: Is there a goal, and is something at risk? If not, don't roll.
Want: what the character wants and how they go about it (one line).
Risk: what failure feels like (one line). Then On the line: the Belief this roll tests, or "none." If one is named, the Danger is a Burden (Section 5.4), and the table may challenge the claim before Set.
Set: the GM sharpens the Danger, checks it can sit beside a success, and names the Difficulty and Danger ranks.
Gather: the software assembles the Skill, any Belief backing, and every shift, shown as one line.
Roll: the software resolves and posts the outcome prompt.
The Danger test: Could this Danger happen even if you get what you want? If not, rephrase it as a cost that can sit beside success: exposure, time, attention, debt, or a misleading detail.
Negotiate before the roll, never after. A player may change approach to change the ranks ("then I'll wait until the secretary leaves"). Once Set closes, the only options are to roll, push, or concede. The one exception is a GM retcon of a quick roll (Section 1.3). If Set isn't agreed in two exchanges, the GM's call stands and the disagreement waits until after the scene.

Skills are rated Untrained to Master. Shifts during Set can take a Skill down to Hampered or up to Heroic and Peerless for one roll. A rating never passes Master; mastery beyond it shows through Traits, Beliefs, and what the fiction allows.
Untrained: anyone may attempt most actions untrained. Some (surgery, magic, an ancient script) need training in the story first.
Difficulty runs Simple to Mythic. The GM may set Legendary or Mythic directly; shifts can bring any Difficulty down to Trivial.
Danger runs Minor to Grave, because Grave is already the worst a cost can be.
Reading guide: Skill and Difficulty on the same row is a contest that leans against the character. One row lower is a fair fight. Two rows lower is routine.
Severity is fixed when Set closes. The Danger rank named at the end of Set, after armor and other shifts, decides the worst cost that can land (Section 6.1). Anything after Set, such as a push, changes only the odds, never the severity.

### 3.4 How the roll works
Skill against Difficulty: a dead heat goes to the opposition. You didn't quite manage it.
Skill against Danger: a dead heat goes to you. You escaped by a hair.
The opposition must beat you to hurt you.
Belief backing (Section 5.1) gives the character a second roll; the better one counts. A roll never has more than one backing.
Silent resolution, dice on demand. The software rolls and posts one outcome prompt. The Ledger shows only ranks and words. Any player may tap a roll to reveal its dice; the GM always sees them.

Failing the goal is not a second punishment. It closes that path or shifts the situation. Only the Danger brings the declared cost. When the goal fails, lean toward outcomes that pressure a Belief or relationship rather than dead ends.
Who writes it: the acting player writes the outcome, success and cost alike. If the cost lands, the GM may first add one sharpening line to the card ("...and the beam pins your left arm"). If the cost lands on someone else (through Cover, Take an ally's Danger, or a shared Danger), that character's player writes their own cost. Success comes first, then the cost.

### 3.6 Success has a voice
The software adds up to three prompts to a success:
Flourish: the roll hits the best result the character's rank allows. The writer adds one true detail that helps the character, in one sentence. (Lower ranks flourish more often; the untrained get lucky.)
Belief success: a Belief backed the roll and the goal succeeds. The Belief must visibly move forward in the same scene: a clue, an opening, a witness moved.
Room to spare: the roll clears the Difficulty by a wide margin. The prompt becomes "You do it with room to spare. What do you do with the room?"
Limits:
A Flourish is true. If the detail should last beyond the scene, it goes into the Canon.
Room to spare is spent inside this post. It can improve how the goal is reached (quieter, faster, with style, or with something noticed on the way), but it can't reach a second goal or cancel a Danger.

### 3.7 Push
After failing the Difficulty, the player may try again once, against the same Difficulty.
A Danger that already hit still happens. The push cannot undo it.
Standard push: if the Danger was avoided, it is checked again one rank worse. You reach further, and the risk grows to meet you.
Conviction push (spend 1 token): the Danger is not checked again. Whatever the first roll decided stands.
A push never takes a Danger past Grave, and never changes its severity (Section 3.3).

### 3.8 Let It Ride
The push is the only immediate retry. After that, the result stands until something real changes: a new approach, tool, helper, or piece of information. A genuine new attempt starts a new prompt card.
Settled questions go into the Canon ("Settled: the vault door can't be forced") so later writers don't contradict them.

### 3.9 Shifts (the only modifiers)
Only a Belief adds a roll. Everything else shifts one rank, named during Set. Shifts stack: a Trait, a fitting tool, and preparation can take an Untrained character to Expert.

### 3.10 Opposed characters and social conflict
Opposed: the opponent's relevant Skill sets the Difficulty on the same row (Capable becomes Demanding, Expert becomes Hard). The declared Danger stays the consequence. The acting side always rolls.
Two PCs: the target's Skill sets the Difficulty, the acting player rolls, and the table agrees on the Danger first. Aric wants to talk Sella out of the duel. Sella's Will is Capable, so the Difficulty is Demanding; the Danger is "she demands the letters back." Aric's player rolls.
Social conflict: same engine. Difficulty is the resistance to the argument; Danger is what the other party demands, learns, or does. Consequences use Standing, Threads, and Burdens. No social hit points.

## 4. Combat: The Battle Roll
A fight is one roll. One player leads and rolls; every other player declares how their character helps or influences the battle, and takes on a risk of their own.

### 4.1 Frame
The GM writes a short Story post that sets the battle and states its stakes: what winning gets (the goal) and what losing costs (the battle's Danger). The opposition sets the Difficulty: "The captain's men hold the barricade: Hard."

### 4.2 Declare
The lead is the character with the most at stake, or whoever the table picks. The lead fills the prompt card as usual: Want, Risk, and any Belief on the line.
Every other player declares one contribution: a line saying what their character does, and which rank it shifts in the side's favor:
Press: shift the lead's Skill up.
Open: shift the Difficulty down.
Cover: shift one character's Danger down.
Every contribution carries its own Danger: the contributor's personal risk ("I'm Wounded by return fire"). Helping in battle is never free.
Contributions can shift any one ladder at most two ranks. The edge rule (Section 3.9) still applies.

### 4.3 Set
The GM sets the Difficulty and every Danger at once. Severity is fixed when Set closes (Section 3.3).

### 4.4 Roll
The software makes one roll: the lead's Skill, backed by the lead's Belief if one applies.
Against the Difficulty, once. The battle is won or lost for everyone.
Against each Danger, separately. Each character's cost lands or not on its own.
Push: the lead may push (Section 3.7). A standard push checks every avoided Danger again one rank worse, so the lead risks the whole table. Before the push, any contributor may withdraw: their shift and their Danger leave the roll (a Danger that already hit still stands). A Conviction push checks none of them again.
Any player may spend Conviction to take an ally's Danger, as usual.

### 4.5 Write
Each player gets an outcome prompt. Each writes a short beat of one to three sentences, and the software stitches the beats into one Story post in the GM's order: the lead's result first, then the contributions, then the costs.

### 4.6 After the battle
The battle is settled (Let It Ride). Another fight needs a real change: reinforcements, a new objective, a pursuit.
A player may concede at Set. A conceding contributor withdraws, and their contribution and Danger both go with them.
Death follows the usual rule: only a Danger declared Grave and Mortal at Set, or escalation from a full Critical slot.
Example. Aric leads the charge on the barricade: a Capable commander, backed by his Belief, against a Hard barricade, with a Severe Danger ("I'm cut down at the gap").

The outcome prompts: the barricade falls. Aric is hit, at worst Wounded. Sella comes through clear. Ilse escapes the spreading fire by a hair.

## 5. Beliefs, Instincts, Traits, Burdens, Conviction
Beliefs drive play: they back a roll when they matter, and they leave a lasting Burden when they are tested.

### 5.1 Beliefs
Each character has three Beliefs: an immediate objective, a relationship, and a worldview. The worldview Belief is the Core Belief.
A Belief holds a conviction and something to do about it:
The duke betrayed my family; I will expose him before the council meets.
Belief backing
A relevant Belief backs you at Trained rank. Test: Would the character still take this action, this way, if the Belief weren't true? If yes, it doesn't apply. The player claims it; the table can challenge.
The Core Belief backs you at Capable rank, but only when it is on the line (Section 5.4). When the Core Belief backs a roll, the Danger is a Burden. The strongest backing comes with the highest stakes.
Backing is a second roll at that rank; the better roll counts. One Belief per roll; if two apply, use the stronger.
Wounds shift the Skill, never the backing.
Invoking a Belief is always free and never earns Conviction.
Belief gives capability, not safety. It improves the chances against both Difficulty and Danger but never changes their ranks.
Rewriting: the objective and relationship Beliefs may be rewritten between sessions. The Core Belief changes only through play: fulfilled, broken, or rewritten when a Burden is laid down.

### 5.2 Instincts
Three per character: automatic behavior ("Never enter a room without checking the exits").
An Instinct happens without a roll or a declaration. The GM can't later spring a surprise it would have prevented.
When an Instinct gets the character into trouble, earn Conviction.
Instincts never back or shift a roll.

### 5.3 Traits
Three to five per character: what the character has become (Merciful, Scarred Veteran, Distrusted by Priests).
A relevant Trait shifts one rank, named at Set. Traits that only change what the story allows need no mechanic.
A harmful Trait earns Conviction when it causes real trouble.
New Traits come from laid-down Burdens, Scars, and an end-of-arc review (the table proposes one Trait per character; the player accepts or declines).

### 5.4 Burdens
When a roll puts a Belief on the line, the declared Danger is a Burden: a lasting change named as a short phrase (Can't Hold a Blade Without Shaking, Doubts the Oath). The character carries it until play lets them lay it down.
On the line means the action itself tests the Belief: acting against it, choosing between it and another Belief, or a cost that strikes at what it protects. Using a Belief to fuel an action does not put it on the line.
A Burden sits on the sheet as a temporary Belief or Instinct and can pull against the others. The GM can bring it in as a Danger whenever it is relevant.
Resolution: a Burden is laid down when a scene tests it at real cost. That moment always earns Conviction, even past the one-per-Belief limit; only the three-token cap still applies.
What it leaves: the player chooses one. The Burden becomes a Trait, or the Belief it came from is rewritten.
At most two active Burdens. A third forces the player to lay down an existing Burden or rewrite the related Belief.
The Risk line says what the Burden is about ("a Burden, about the fire"); the player names it after the roll, once they see how it landed. The optional Burden oracle (Section 13.1) can help. At campaign start the table agrees on themes to handle lightly or leave out.
Example. Mara carries Doubts the Oath from the night she let the smuggler go. In the riot she could look away from a deserter; instead she shields him, and the captain sees her do it. That is a real cost, so she lays down the Burden and earns Conviction. She chooses a Trait: Keeps the Oath She Chose.

### 5.5 Conviction
Earned when acting on a Belief costs something real:
pursuing a Belief when an easier or safer path was available;
accepting a serious Danger or sacrifice because of a Belief;
choosing one Belief over another;
a Belief fulfilled, broken, or rewritten through play;
an Instinct or harmful Trait causing real trouble;
laying down a Burden;
conceding against a Belief;
losing a significant possession at real cost;
helping and sharing a Danger at real cost.
Spent on:
Conviction push: the Danger isn't checked again (Section 3.7).
Take an ally's Danger: move another character's declared Danger onto your own character.
Limits: start with 1; carry over between sessions; hold at most 3; earn at most one per Belief per session, except for laying down a Burden (Section 5.4). The GM awards tokens; any player may nominate another player's moment. Awards go in the Codex's Conviction log.

There are no hit points. A wound is a named injury on a four-rung ladder, and the Danger rank decides how bad it can get.

Death is always telegraphed. Mortal comes only from a Grave Danger declared Mortal at Set, or from escalation when Critical is full.
One roll decides both the goal and the injury. There are no separate attack and damage rolls.
The ladder is physical. Social and emotional harm use Danger, Burdens, Standing, and Threads.
Exhaustion (fatigue, hunger, magical drain) fills Hurt and Wounded slots and escalates, but stops at Collapsed: the character can't act until rested. It is Mortal only when declared Grave (freezing, starvation).
Lasting conditions (Haunted, Corrupted) are Burdens or Traits, not wounds.

### 6.2 Wound effects
A wound matters only when it is relevant to the action, agreed at Set and never after.
Hurt: shift the related Skill down a rank.
Wounded: shift the related Skill down a rank; when the action strains the injury, shift the Danger up a rank.
Critical: directly impaired actions may drop to Untrained or Hampered; some may be impossible.
Mortal: dying; needs stabilizing.
Belief backing is never affected by wounds.

### 6.3 Escalation, armor, weapons
Escalation. An overflowing wound climbs to the next open rung: a Hurt with full Hurt and Wounded rows becomes Critical. The player renames escalated wounds (Bruised Ribs becomes Cracked Ribs). When Critical is full, the GM warns at Set: "Any wound now could be Mortal."
Armor shifts the Danger down a rank at Set, against threats it can stop. Because severity is fixed at Set, this also lowers the worst wound.

Weapons set the Danger rank they threaten. A weapon that suits the situation (reach against a charge, a shield against arrows) shifts one rank.

Safe rest means the GM would not declare a Danger for that night.

Stabilizing a Mortal wound is always a roll. Failing the goal means death. The Danger is a cost to the healer, and the healer may push.
Scars (optional): a fully healed Critical or Mortal wound may become a Scar Trait (Walks with a Cane).

### 6.5 Concession
A player may concede at Set (after seeing the ranks and odds, before rolling) or instead of pushing.
A Danger that already hit still happens; conceding stops things getting worse.
The conceding player writes the defeat, choosing from: surrender, escape, capture, disarmament, humiliation, loss of the objective, separation, injury, forced agreement, lost reputation, a debt owed, or a significant possession lost. The GM cannot make it worse than the declared Danger.
Conceding against a Belief earns Conviction.
NPCs can concede and offer terms.

## 7. Character Creation
Characters are written, not built: each step is prose in the Story pane, and the other players turn that prose into the sheet.

### 7.1 Sequence
The GM adds one table-wide world fact: the setting's central pressure.
Origin. A short paragraph. Gain one Skill at Trained and one Connection (family, home, or community).
Three Life Chapters. One paragraph each, with a one-line summary: where I was, what I did, how it ended. A fourth for veteran or older characters.
World facts. One per chapter.
Crossing paths. Two crossings per character.
Capital. Wealth, Network, Standing words, up to three possessions.
Beliefs. Three, written from the chapters and crossings; one marked Core.
Instincts. Three.
Starting hook. The past Thread that has caught up with the character today.
End-of-creation check: every character has a Skill at Capable or better, Connections to two other characters, and a Thread the GM can use. The table fixes any gap before play.

### 7.2 Life Chapters
After each chapter is posted:
The other players grant one Skill (new at Trained, or an existing Skill raised a rank) and one Trait, based on what the prose shows.
The writing player picks one of Connection, Resource, or Thread. Across all chapters the character needs at least one of each.
One swap: the writing player may swap one granted Skill or Trait during all of creation.
Starting Skills cap at Expert.
Chapters are build-your-own, with example lists.

### 7.3 The bad chapter
One chapter must end badly. It is the second or third chapter, not the most recent.
It must leave a Thread (an enemy, debt, promise, or secret).
The other players grant a harmful Trait.
If the bad ending tested a Belief, the player may start with one Burden (it counts toward the limit of two).
The other players propose how it ends badly; the writing player picks one. No random tables.

### 7.4 Crossing paths
Written after the chapters as short Story-pane scenes. The other character's player may borrow the viewpoint to answer.
Each crossing names a specific chapter from each character.
Each produces a Connection for both characters and at most one Thread between them.
Everyone has two crossings; nobody is left out. One crossing should touch one of the character's Beliefs.
With the table's agreement, one link may stay a stranger the story hasn't resolved yet.

### 7.5 World facts
One per chapter and one per crossing (about five per character), kept in the Canon.
Each fact leaves a question open ("The Border War ended in a treaty nobody trusts").
The GM can sharpen a fact but not erase it. In a conflict, the earlier fact wins unless both players agree to change it.
A fact that names a community is the moment to give the characters involved a Standing word.

## 8. Resources, Standing, Possessions
Wealth and Network are ranked like Skills; Standing is a word; possessions are Traits attached to objects.

### 8.1 Wealth and Network
Both start at the second rung (Comfortable; A circle of acquaintances). A Resource output from a chapter can raise either a rank. Each rung rolls like the Skill rank beside it.

Wealth rolls only for big purchases or raising money under pressure. Danger: debt or attention. If it hits, Wealth drops a rung or the character takes a debt Thread. Wealth recovers through play, not time.
Network rolls find people and call in favors. Danger: owing something back, or exposure.
A resource takes the place of the Skill when the action is about that resource.

### 8.2 Standing
One word per community: Distrusted, Unknown, Known, Respected.
Respected shifts the Difficulty down a rank for requests inside that community; Distrusted shifts it up.
Known and Unknown change what the story allows.

### 8.3 Significant possessions
Ordinary gear is assumed. Only significant items are tracked, at most three.
A possession is a Trait attached to an object: when relevant, it shifts one rank.
A possession can be the Danger ("if you fail, the sword is taken"). Losing one at real cost earns Conviction.
Recorded as one line of story: "Father's Sword: carried through the Border War; the duke wants it back."
Followers, estates, and ships follow the same rule.

## 9. Magic
Magic uses the same roll; what makes a tradition distinct is its Method (what it needs) and its Price (what its Danger costs).

### 9.1 Traditions
A tradition is one of the character's Life Chapters. The granted Skill is the magical Skill; the granted Trait is the tradition's mark (Hedge Witch).
One tradition per character for the playtest.
Recorded in the Roster as three lines: Source, Method, Price.
Hedge Witch. Source: the old places. Method: herbs, knots, spoken charms. Price: owes the land a favor.
Fiction gate: if the Method isn't available in the scene, the character can't cast. No roll.
Using an enchanted item or being targeted by a spell needs no tradition. A tradition means you can work magic.

### 9.2 Resolution
The Danger is the tradition's Price, shaped to the moment and declared at Set.
Bigger effects mean a bigger Difficulty; more reckless effects mean a bigger Danger.
A spell lasts the scene unless the Difficulty or Danger says otherwise.
Physical backlash uses the wound ladder; backlash that changes the caster is a Burden.
Ritual magic is preparation: it shifts ranks in the caster's favor instead of adding a roll.
Opposed magic uses the opposed-characters rule (Section 3.10).
A push works as usual (Section 3.7). The Price is a Danger like any other.

### 9.3 Price severities
Each tradition lists its Price at three severities, matching the Danger ladder:

Danger |

The Price is limited to what the tradition lists. Corruption, haunting, and transformation are Burdens, not points. The GM raises the severity at Set for large effects or repeated casting.

### 9.4 Practiced and improvised effects
Practiced: up to three, recorded in the Roster (Wall of Flame, Calm the Beasts). Shifts the Difficulty down a rank.
Improvised: anything new within the Method. Shifts both Difficulty and Danger up a rank.
An improvised effect that succeeds and matters may become practiced. A fourth replaces one the player gives up.
Disputes about what fits a tradition are settled by the Canon.

### 9.5 No magic points
A caster can always attempt magic; the cost is whatever the Danger names.

Exhaustion stops at Collapsed, so magic can't kill by exhaustion alone. Rest clears exhaustion; only play lays down Burdens.
Powerful magic needs a fictional prerequisite, recorded as a Thread ("obtain a true name from a living dragon"). Meeting it unlocks the effect as practiced.

## 11. Rules of Thumb and Central Thesis

### 11.1 Rules of thumb
If there is no Danger, do not roll.
Before rolling, state the goal and the Danger. The player says what failure feels like; the GM sharpens it and sets the ranks.
Difficulty measures reaching the goal. Danger measures avoiding the cost.
Belief gives leverage, not immunity.
Shift a rank instead of stacking modifiers. The only extra roll is Belief backing, one per roll.
A wound is something that happened to you, not a number you lost.
Healing keeps consequences without benching the player.
Character history must leave usable things behind.
Every important rule answers: What can I do? Why do I care? What am I risking? What can the GM put under pressure?
Use the same core mechanic unless a new subsystem produces something it can't.
Change creates future story, through Burdens, Scars, and rewritten Beliefs, not only bigger numbers.
Failing the goal shifts the situation. Only the Danger brings the declared cost.
Conviction is earned by paying for a Belief, not by playing it.
When a Belief is on the line, the cost is lasting, and play resolves it.
No one is surprised by a cost. It is declared at Set, its severity can't grow after, and death is always visible.
Automate the remembering, never the narrating. The software tracks and rolls; players write.
If a roll's meaning can't be said in one sentence, the mechanic is too complex.

### 11.2 Central thesis
Build a person through their history. Give that person explicit beliefs about what matters. Put those beliefs under pressure. Before every important action, tell the player both what is difficult and what is dangerous. Let the player name what failure would feel like, and choose whether conviction is worth the risk. Test the character's Skill against separate Difficulty and Danger. Let a Belief tested at real cost leave a lasting Burden. Let success, failure, wounds, relationships, resources, and magic permanently change the character's story.
ACTION: What can I bring to this?
DIFFICULTY: Can I accomplish what I intend?
DANGER: Can I avoid what I knowingly risked?
BELIEF: Why am I willing to try anyway?
CONVICTION: What am I willing to pay to push?

## 12. First Playtest Scope
Play Sections 1–10 as written, with ranks over flat dice and one simple magical tradition if needed.
Do not add: Skill advancement; match/twist rules; progress clocks or long-term projects; factions; nonphysical condition ladders; separate social or magic combat; equipment lists or encumbrance; detailed spell construction; any digital option in Section 13.
